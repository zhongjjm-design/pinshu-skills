# Business visual mothers

These seven method families are available as editorial guidance. They are not seven extra registered rendering modes. Use them when they materially fit a business report, then describe the visual decision explicitly in the prompt. No private example images or external rendering engines are bundled.


## Typographic Metaphor

## Identity

Make the key Chinese word participate in the argument. The type is not a headline placed above an illustration; its stroke, counterspace, cut, repetition, compression, or alignment becomes the information structure.

Use for:

- a cover or chapter opener with one memorable thesis
- a concept page with one tension, threshold, shift, or contrast
- a short social graphic whose first job is recognition

Avoid for dense evidence, multi-variable comparison, and exact analytical charts.


## Composition families

Choose one family; do not repeat the same family across every page.

1. `stroke-as-structure`: a character stroke becomes a threshold, path, container, or fault line.
2. `counterspace-as-opportunity`: the interior negative space carries the key evidence or transition.
3. `split-state`: one word is divided into current and target states by a meaningful cut.
4. `sequence`: controlled repetition of one word shows acceleration, decay, scale, or convergence.

## Typography

- Preserve natural Chinese character proportions.
- Prefer custom spacing, masking, cropping, or stroke extension over horizontal compression.
- Keep the complete original title in smaller type when the hero word is abbreviated.
- Use at most one display family and one restrained text family.

## Color and material

- Use one quiet base, one dark typographic mass, and one semantic accent.
- Let whitespace and scale create drama; avoid gradients used only for spectacle.
- Thin rules, registration marks, or restrained paper texture may support the composition.

## Prompt kernel

> Create a restrained Chinese business visual in the typographic-metaphor mother. Make "[core phrase]" the main information geometry using [composition family]. Preserve natural Chinese width. Integrate the single metaphor "[metaphor]" into the letterform itself. Keep the full title "[complete title]" readable in smaller type. Use [palette], generous whitespace, and precise editorial annotations. No ordinary PPT title layout, no decorative icons, no fake condensed Chinese, no unrelated metaphor objects.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- the hero word is merely large and does not explain the thesis
- the main word becomes illegible without delivering compensating meaning
- the text has been unnaturally squeezed
- decorative objects carry a different metaphor from the typography
- five or more modules compete with the hero word


## Strategic Map

## Identity

Turn a strategic choice into a navigable field. Regions, axes, boundaries, vectors, and opportunity zones must correspond to real decision variables rather than decorative geography.

Use for:

- positioning, competitive landscape, portfolio choice
- risk-return, maturity-value, control-speed, or other two-variable decisions
- opportunity spaces, strategic migration, and priority zones

Avoid when axes cannot be defined honestly or the content is only a linear process.


## Composition families

1. `coordinate field`: two defined axes, named zones, current position, target position, and one movement vector.
2. `terrain bands`: layered opportunity, constraint, and risk bands with explicit boundary logic.
3. `migration route`: a starting zone, decision gates, detours, and a target zone inside one map system.
4. `portfolio constellation`: a small set of positioned options with size, color, or ring encoding one additional variable.

## Information rules

- Name both axes and state their direction.
- Distinguish observed position, inferred opportunity, and recommended move.
- Use no more than five principal regions.
- Put the conclusion next to the decisive boundary or movement, not in a detached box.
- If coordinates are illustrative, label them as conceptual.

## Typography and color

- Use compact labels, fine rules, and one strong strategic accent.
- Keep the background quiet enough that zones remain readable.
- Use opacity and line weight to separate field, boundary, route, and annotation.

## Prompt kernel

> Build a 16:9 strategic-map business visual. The decision field uses X-axis “[x variable]” and Y-axis “[y variable]”. Show [current position], [target zone], [constraint boundary], and one recommended movement vector. Use a restrained consulting palette with precise Chinese labels and generous negative space. The map must express the decision logic even without decoration. No fantasy map, no invented metrics, no unexplained axes, no random icons.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- axes, regions, or distances have no semantic definition
- it resembles a transit map but does not support a decision
- too many arrows create multiple reading paths
- conceptual positions look like measured data without disclosure
- the page is actually a process and should use another mother


## Structural Section

## Identity

Explain a business system as a load-bearing section: foundation, layers, supports, interfaces, and visible outcomes. Architecture is information logic, not decoration.

Use for:

- capability systems, operating models, organization and governance
- business foundations, maturity stacks, and platform layers
- dependencies between resources, mechanisms, and outcomes

Avoid when layers are merely a list with no dependency, load, or support relationship.


## Composition families

1. `foundation-to-outcome`: foundations carry operating layers and a visible value surface.
2. `cutaway system`: one section reveals hidden mechanisms behind the customer-facing layer.
3. `bridge span`: two sides are connected by capabilities, governance, or enabling systems.
4. `load path`: value, risk, or accountability moves through columns, joints, and supports.

## Information rules

- Every layer must answer what it supports and what supports it.
- Use three to five structural levels.
- Name critical joints, gaps, or weak supports.
- Make the conclusion a structural diagnosis: missing foundation, overloaded span, weak interface, or viable build sequence.
- Do not imply physical precision where the structure is conceptual.

## Typography and material

- Use section labels, level marks, fine dimensions, and restrained annotations.
- Favor warm neutral, graphite, off-white, and one structural accent.
- Use solid, hatch, void, and transparency consistently to encode material roles.

## Prompt kernel

> Create a restrained business-system structural section. Show [foundation], [operating layers], [critical interfaces], and [visible outcome] as one load-bearing architecture. Use [composition family], three to five levels, precise Chinese labels, section marks, and one accent that identifies the key weakness or leverage point. No decorative skyscraper, no generic pyramid, no impossible perspective, no unrelated icons.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- the structure could be replaced by an unordered bullet list
- layers do not support or depend on one another
- architectural realism overwhelms information
- all components have equal visual weight
- a generic house or building is used without business meaning


## Editorial Collage

## Identity

Build an argument from evidence fragments: photography, documents, annotations, numbers, captions, and deliberate cropping. It should feel edited, not decorated.

Use for:

- report openers, industry observation, trend narratives
- mixed qualitative evidence and visual context
- human, market, cultural, or operational stories needing texture

Avoid when exact comparison, auditability, or a clean system diagram is primary.


## Composition families

1. `evidence wall`: one dominant image plus two or three annotated evidence fragments.
2. `document splice`: report excerpts, highlighted lines, stamps, and one interpretive headline.
3. `before-after archive`: two temporal states joined by dates, captions, and a decisive transition.
4. `field-note spread`: contact-sheet rhythm, marginal notes, and a numbered observation sequence.

## Image and sourcing rules

- Give every fragment a role: context, proof, contrast, or consequence.
- Prefer user-supplied or properly sourced material.
- Do not imitate a living publication’s exact layout, masthead, or proprietary marks.
- Keep source notes for photographs and documents when required.
- Generated documentary imagery must be labeled as illustrative when it could be mistaken for evidence.

## Typography and color

- Use editorial hierarchy: headline, deck, caption, folio, source.
- Mix at most two type voices.
- Use a restrained paper, ink, or newsprint system with one signal color.
- Cropping may create tension; it must not obscure the thesis.

## Prompt kernel

> Design an editorial-collage business visual around the argument “[thesis]”. Use one dominant [image role], two or three sourced evidence fragments, concise Chinese captions, a numbered reading path, and a restrained [paper/ink] palette. Make each fragment serve context, proof, contrast, or consequence. No mood-board randomness, no fake logos, no invented documents, no scrapbook stickers, no generic stock-business people.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- the page is a mood board with no argument
- fragments lack captions or evidence roles
- decoration is mistaken for documentary proof
- more than five competing fragments destroy hierarchy
- the layout copies a known publication too literally


## Engineering Blueprint Narrative

## Identity

Explain how a system works through components, interfaces, flows, states, and control boundaries. The blueprint must tell an operational story rather than merely signal “technology”.

Use for:

- AI systems, automation, workflows, platforms, and agent architectures
- operating mechanisms, handoffs, feedback loops, and exception handling
- technical-commercial relationships that need both mechanism and outcome

Avoid when the content is only a slogan or when no components and interfaces can be named.


## Composition families

1. `input-engine-output`: inputs enter a mechanism, pass controls, and produce measurable outputs.
2. `exploded assembly`: modules separate spatially while interfaces and ownership remain clear.
3. `state machine`: a small set of operating states, transitions, gates, and failure paths.
4. `closed-loop system`: sensing, decision, execution, measurement, and correction form one traceable loop.

## Information rules

- Name system boundary, inputs, outputs, controls, and human checkpoints.
- Use line styles consistently for data, action, feedback, and exception.
- Show where responsibility changes hands.
- Include failure or override paths when risk matters.
- Do not add meaningless code, pseudo-interfaces, or random technical numbers.

## Typography and color

- Use precise labels, monospaced accents only for real identifiers, and strong hierarchy.
- Favor off-white or dark graphite with cyan, orange, or green used semantically.
- Use schematic symbols sparingly and consistently.

## Prompt kernel

> Create an engineering-blueprint narrative for “[system]”. Use [composition family] to show system boundary, [inputs], [modules], [interfaces], human checkpoints, exception path, and [outputs]. Encode data, action, feedback, and risk with consistent line styles. Use exact Chinese labels and a restrained technical palette. No sci-fi glow, no robot head, no meaningless code, no decorative circuit board, no invented interface labels.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- the drawing says “AI” without explaining a mechanism
- lines cross without direction or ownership
- technical decoration exceeds operational content
- no system boundary or human checkpoint exists
- the result could not support a narrated walkthrough


## Chinese Modernism

## Identity

Use Chinese typographic rhythm, editorial restraint, asymmetry, vertical-horizontal tension, and print structure to carry a contemporary business argument. Cultural cues must arise from language and composition, not ornamental symbols.

Use for:

- Chinese brands, cultural commerce, local strategy, and long-term narratives
- restrained covers, chapter openers, and point-of-view pages
- content where Chinese language itself should lead the visual identity

Avoid when dense global market data or highly technical system mechanics are primary.


## Composition families

1. `vertical thesis`: one vertical headline axis with horizontal evidence and large quiet fields.
2. `seal-and-grid`: a strict modern grid interrupted by one small semantic red block or seal-like index.
3. `folded spread`: two editorial planes create dialogue between tradition/current state or idea/action.
4. `ink-weight contrast`: dense black typographic mass faces a sparse field of annotations and conclusion.

## Typography and cultural rules

- Let Song, Hei, or Kai qualities serve hierarchy; do not mix them as costume.
- Use vertical text only when the reading order remains obvious.
- Preserve punctuation and natural Chinese spacing.
- Use red, black, off-white, mineral gray, or muted green with restraint.
- Avoid dragons, lanterns, calligraphy splashes, fake seals, and “oriental” ornament unless the content specifically requires them.

## Prompt kernel

> Design a contemporary Chinese-modernist business visual for “[thesis]”. Use [composition family], restrained Song/Hei contrast, deliberate vertical-horizontal rhythm, large off-white negative space, black ink mass, and one small semantic red accent. Keep the complete Chinese title accurate and naturally proportioned. Express cultural confidence through typography and grid, not traditional ornament. No fake calligraphy, no lanterns, no decorative ink splash, no generic national-style poster.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- cultural identity depends on clichés rather than structure
- vertical text creates an ambiguous reading order
- the result looks nostalgic instead of contemporary
- too many typefaces or red accents become festive decoration
- the business conclusion is lost in atmosphere


## Data Journalism

## Identity

Lead with verified evidence. Charts, annotations, context, sources, and a conclusion-led headline form the visual argument. The design must allow the reader to distinguish measured facts from interpretation.

Use for:

- trends, markets, operations, surveys, benchmarks, and business performance
- evidence-led report pages and social explainers
- questions involving change over time, composition, distribution, ranking, or correlation

Do not use when no trustworthy dataset exists. Route a conceptual claim to another mother.


## Composition families

1. `one-chart story`: one decisive chart, direct annotations, context band, and one conclusion.
2. `small multiples`: repeated scales compare segments, regions, periods, or scenarios.
3. `evidence stack`: headline number, main chart, two supporting indicators, methodology, and source.
4. `annotated timeline`: time series with turning points, causal context, and clearly separated inference.

## Data rules

- Record source URL or document, access/publication date, units, period, sample, and methodology.
- Keep common scales for comparisons unless a scale change is explicitly disclosed.
- Start bars at zero unless a non-zero baseline is necessary and conspicuously marked.
- Use direct labels where possible; keep legends close to the data.
- Label estimates, forecasts, and derived calculations.
- Never let a decorative metaphor distort magnitude.

## Production boundary

- Raster image generation may explore art direction and chart composition.
- Build final dense, audit-critical, or editable charts with a charting, slide, spreadsheet, or design tool.
- Compare every visible value and proportion with the structured source before approval.

## Typography and color

- Use a conclusion-led headline, compact annotation style, and readable source note.
- Reserve accent color for the evidence that answers the question.
- Use neutral comparison colors and accessible contrast.

## Prompt kernel

> Create a data-journalism business visual answering “[question]” with the conclusion “[headline]”. Use [composition family] and only the supplied dataset. Show exact units, dates, values, direct annotations, methodology, and source note. Separate observed facts from interpretation and forecasts. Use a restrained newsroom palette with one evidence accent. No invented numbers, no decorative 3D chart, no unlabeled axis, no misleading baseline, no pseudo-source.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- any number, unit, date, or source is altered
- a chart cannot be reconstructed from the supplied data
- visual proportions contradict the values
- annotations imply causation unsupported by evidence
- the final needs precision or editability but only a generated bitmap exists


## Metaphor Router

Choose a metaphor only after selecting the visual mother.

| Metaphor | Best relationship |
|---|---|
| funnel | selection, conversion, narrowing |
| path | progression, roadmap, transformation |
| ladder | upgrading, maturity, pricing tiers |
| matrix | comparison, priority, portfolio choice |
| coordinate field | positioning, opportunity zone, risk-return |
| flywheel | recurrence, compounding, network effect |
| node network | collaboration, architecture, resource relationships |
| data flow | automation, inputs and outputs, system movement |
| threshold | qualification, stage gate, decision criteria |
| window | timing, market opening, breakthrough |
| defense line | risk, governance, compliance, safety |
| fault line | structural change, disruption, generational break |
| container | resource pool, value pool, business model |
| compass | direction, strategic judgment, navigation |
| building structure | capability foundation, organization, governance |

## Abstraction level

- Default to information geometry, not literal scenery.
- Use literal objects only when the selected mother is editorial collage or engineering blueprint narrative and the object materially explains the mechanism.
- A threshold can be a line, cut, pressure surface, or decision rule; it does not have to be a physical gate.
- A building structure must express dependency or load, not merely look architectural.

## One-metaphor test

Reject a composition when removing one visual object would reveal that the remaining objects belong to unrelated metaphor families.
