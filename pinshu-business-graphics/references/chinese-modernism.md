# Chinese Modernism

## Identity

Use Chinese typographic rhythm, editorial restraint, asymmetry, vertical-horizontal tension, and print structure to carry a contemporary business argument. Cultural cues must arise from language and composition, not ornamental symbols.

Use for:

- Chinese brands, cultural commerce, local strategy, and long-term narratives
- restrained covers, chapter openers, and point-of-view pages
- content where Chinese language itself should lead the visual identity

Avoid when dense global market data or highly technical system mechanics are primary.


## Composition families

1. `vertical thesis`: one vertical headline axis with horizontal evidence and large quiet fields.
2. `seal-and-grid`: a strict modern grid interrupted by one small semantic red block or seal-like index.
3. `folded spread`: two editorial planes create dialogue between tradition/current state or idea/action.
4. `ink-weight contrast`: dense black typographic mass faces a sparse field of annotations and conclusion.

## Typography and cultural rules

- Let Song, Hei, or Kai qualities serve hierarchy; do not mix them as costume.
- Use vertical text only when the reading order remains obvious.
- Preserve punctuation and natural Chinese spacing.
- Use red, black, off-white, mineral gray, or muted green with restraint.
- Avoid dragons, lanterns, calligraphy splashes, fake seals, and “oriental” ornament unless the content specifically requires them.

## Prompt kernel

> Design a contemporary Chinese-modernist business visual for “[thesis]”. Use [composition family], restrained Song/Hei contrast, deliberate vertical-horizontal rhythm, large off-white negative space, black ink mass, and one small semantic red accent. Keep the complete Chinese title accurate and naturally proportioned. Express cultural confidence through typography and grid, not traditional ornament. No fake calligraphy, no lanterns, no decorative ink splash, no generic national-style poster.

## Failure boundaries

Reject when:

- cultural identity depends on clichés rather than structure
- vertical text creates an ambiguous reading order
- the result looks nostalgic instead of contemporary
- too many typefaces or red accents become festive decoration
- the business conclusion is lost in atmosphere
