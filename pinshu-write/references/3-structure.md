# Step 4: Structure (types, titles, subheads, outline)

The writer produces the one-line judgment, three titles, the subheads, the outline and the opening paragraph (prompt 1, outline mode); the structure editor checks them (prompt 4). The author sees the result at stop 2. In step 7 the managing editor reads the finished draft as a whole (`5-review.md`).

Readers decide in this order: the title makes them open the article; the subheads, the opening and the ending decide whether they keep reading. Most of the effort goes here, because model reviewers catch errors but not emptiness.

## Article type

The organizer proposes the type from the seed; stop 2 shows it and the author may change it. "Free form" skips the type but keeps the hard rules and the review. Every type shares one spine: one main line (ranked dimensions answering the same question still count as one), an opening that puts the reader inside their own situation, sections that each carry one step of the argument, and an ending that gives the reader something to do or a judgment to keep.

| # | Type | Structure | Pass test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Real people and events | Concrete action; how it came about; one classic principle explains it; where it stops working; what the reader can do | Remove the principle and the story still stands |
| 2 | Checklist the reader can use | The failure scene; why usual practice does not prevent it; 5 to 9 checkable items, each with a reason and the moment to use it; where the list does not apply | The list alone is usable; someone has done every item |
| 3 | Show the work | Finished result first; the bill (money, time, versions); where it broke; at least two things that did not work; who it suits | First-hand test evidence with numbers, including failures |
| 4 | Owner's words against the books | One quote (occasion, date); 3 to 5 numbers on one basis; do words and numbers match; judgment | Every number traces to a filing or public statement; quotes unaltered and apart from commentary |
| 5 | After the news | The fact in one sentence; what it means for owners, marketers, employees; how often this has happened; the next signal; judgment and confidence | Checked against the original; "what it means for you" is never relaxed |
| 6 | What the customer actually sees | The scene in front of the customer; who the customer is; one brand principle; observable results; can peers borrow it | Written from the customer's side, not a press-release rewrite |
| 7 | A classic, reread | The classic judgment and its era; the conditions under which it held; today's case; check each condition; the corrected judgment | The classic can fail the test; exact source |
| 8 | Short emotional piece | A conflict image; one person, one event; a turn; one line people repeat | Not judged by this Skill |
| 9 | AI trends, applications, reviews | One-sentence conclusion; which real tasks were tested; where it wins and loses; who it suits; versions and dates | Failed tests reported; the judgment is current; version, date, task and cost stated |
| 10 | Integration piece | The reader's question; the best answers from the most-read articles, each credited by author and outlet; where they agree and disagree; our judgment and choices; what the reader does with it | Every borrowed point credited in the body and listed after it; quotes short, summaries our own; our judgment, not the sources, closes the article |

Type 10 is chosen when the angle is taken and we have no angle or experience of our own.

**Publishing form**, settled at stop 2 with the type: an illustrated article (the usual form, subheads, the account's length); a plain-text short piece (about 1,000 Chinese characters or less, no subheads, ten paragraphs or fewer, one point made fully); or a Xiaohongshu-style image post (handed to the account's visual Skill). An article written for one form does not fit another, so the form is decided before drafting.

**Practical how-to pieces** usually combine type 1 or 5 with type 2: a real scene, why it happens from the other party's side, three or four concrete moves with real cases, the limits, and an ending that hands over something usable at the moment it is needed (a sentence to say, a table to send, a first step). Their judgment is a thread, one sentence that ties the steps together, not a thesis to defend; counter question 5 does not apply, and questions 1 and 2 test whether the steps are new. The outline becomes a route table: each step, who has actually done it and showed their work, the source, whether we have done it, where we fall short. A step must have been done by someone and be sourced; single-source steps are marked optional. When the agent merges several people's steps into one route, each step shows which sources mention it. The author's own rejected drafts and comments never appear in the article as evidence.

**Style cards** are optional and borrow another writer's structure and moves only, never identity, catchphrases or experiences. At most two per article; the account profile wins on conflict.

## The article the author chose

First check of every outline: put the author's own words for the direction and the seed beside the title candidates and subheads, and answer: would a reader who sees only the title expect this article, or a different one? A reader's pass only confirms that a title is clear; this check confirms it is the article the author picked. A different article means rebuilding the outline from the direction before polishing any wording.

## The body: something the reader can act on

- For each section: can the reader open a tool and do one thing right away after reading it?
- A "how it is made" piece names the tools, the number of steps, the money and time, and the step where people usually get stuck; concepts are only the frame.
- Hands-on material, the author's and other practitioners', is the main ingredient; the judgment must not squeeze it out.
- For business readers, order sections by the questions they need answered: what stays the same, what keeps working for years, what to keep doing, what it pays back. Technical or academic numbers never carry a section alone.
- Outside evidence follows the budget in `4-writing.md`.

## The outline pushes forward

- Pick the order first. Opinion pieces: a sharp or counter-intuitive judgment the reader has not heard goes first and the sections unfold it; a familiar judgment goes question first, the answer completing at the end ("Opening, concept, arc, ending"). Practical and news pieces: answer first or conflict first.
- Vertical: each section is the "why" or "how" the reader naturally asks after the previous one.
- Horizontal: parallel items are the same kind of thing, in a stated order (Barbara Minto, The Pyramid Principle).
- Chain read: the first and last sentence of every section, read in a row, form one argument flowing downhill. Sections that only sit side by side are notes.
- Cut check: find the natural ending, where the reader's feeling first peaks; what follows is another article, so cut it or shrink it to a third.
- Read the title and subheads alone, in order: they tell the whole argument.

## Titles

The first test for every title and subhead is whether a reader can tell what the next part will say from the heading alone, without reading the body. Then every title and subhead is judged in this fixed order; when a later quality conflicts with an earlier one, the later gives way.

1. **Direct**: a reader who sees only this line knows what it gives them.
2. **Accurate**: it states exactly what the section delivers, no more.
3. **Memorable**: where possible one short phrase the reader can keep (a named rule, a named step).
4. **Interesting**: a fresh or counter-intuitive edge, only if 1 to 3 hold.

**Hard test, on every title and subhead before the author sees them.** Cover the body. Show only the line to someone outside the industry (or a fresh model with no context): "In one sentence, what will this teach you, or claim?" A wrong, vague or guessed answer means rewrite.

Title rules:

- States plainly what the article is about, ideally the claim or the reader's own question; a practical piece names the result the reader will produce.
- **Every noun can be pointed at by someone who has not read the body.** Words the article defines for itself ("the second time", "the ruler") fail.
- **Metaphors are earned in the body first**; they never appear for the first time in a title or subhead.
- **Distinctive, not only clear**: it would fit only this article. A line that would fit any article fails whatever its form: a general call, "in the age of AI, X" with nothing specific, a neat couplet that says nothing. A couplet or a three-part line that states this article's own point is fine. Distinctiveness comes from this article's judgment, number, event or reader question.
- **Three candidates in three different forms** (one states the conclusion, one is the reader's own question, one rests on a concrete number or event), the recommended one marked with a reason in one line. The author picks.
- 15 to 25 Chinese characters; vague words ("many", "key") become a number or object. Questions, suspense, contrast and the marks 「」？……！ are fair tools as long as a reader still sees at a glance what the article is about; a title is direct and still makes the reader want to click. Not the seed's original wording, not an abstract judgment, no reversal that needs the body, no key word with a second everyday meaning.
- **Trending words the reader knows stay in.** A product or model name central to the body belongs in the title.
- Candidates may come from `gzh-title-strategist` if installed; the account's own rules win. If a market reference page exists, the titles of its small-account big-response articles sit beside our candidates with their ratios, for reference: whether a title resembles a hit is not the test; the test is whether a stranger seeing it would open it and, having opened it, not feel cheated.
- Chinese examples of every title and subhead pattern are printed by `python3 scripts/audit_draft.py --rules`.

## Subheads

- A subhead says what its section really claims and makes the reader want to read it. A line that sounds neat but says nothing fails, whatever its form; couplets and three-part lines are fine when they carry the claim.
- A subhead reads as a natural sentence, with a comma where speech would pause, within sixteen Chinese characters, and carries no number ("01").
- Pattern by type: practical (the action plus why it matters, ideally one named rule; no numbered steps or sequence words); opinion (the section's claim outright, not a topic label, riddle or question); method (the method in one line); news analysis (what this event means for the reader); integration (what the reader learns from this group of sources, stated as a claim or method).
- Name the subject and what happens. Every word survives "where does this word come from?" No coined term, unexplained metaphor or pronoun ("this", "it", "the second one") that needs the section to make sense.
- **No line that can be split the wrong way**: ask whether any two characters can group into a different word. An outsider's misreading is a must-fix.
- No worn-out abstract words (judgment, cognition, underlying logic; the audit warns); use the words of the reader's own day.
- Read alone, in order, the subheads tell the whole argument. Similar in length; never pad one or strip its punctuation to reach a number.
- Business pieces for owners and managers use subheads as the skim skeleton; personal long-form pieces about lived experience may go without.

## Opening, concept, arc, ending

- **Opening of an opinion piece** depends on the judgment. A sharp or counter-intuitive judgment the reader has never heard opens head-on, set by one punchline, and the sections then unfold it layer by layer: the surprise is the reason to read on. A judgment the reader has heard before stays back: start from a feeling or a scene the reader shares ("I wonder if you have felt this too") and raise the question; the sections reveal the answer one layer at a time and complete it at the end, because a familiar answer given up front ends the reading. Cases serve as evidence inside the body; only a news story of the day opens an article. The first sentence names its subject. At most one hypothetical scene per article, and the audit requires a fix when the opening is built on "suppose" or "imagine". Choose a kind different from the account's last few openings. No hook-pain-promise formula. The fear of not knowing or of falling behind is a fair hook when the article answers it with a judgment and a way to act; fear raised and left unanswered fails. Glance test: someone who read only the opening can say what the article claims or what question it will answer.
- **Opening of other types**: a news-analysis piece opens on the day's event; a practical piece on the failure scene; a show-the-work piece on the finished result. Within three paragraphs the reader is inside the problem.
- **A central new concept** shows what it looks like the first time it appears: first what it did in the example, in everyday words, then its name.
- **Arc**: opening, development, turn and close are each identifiable. Development explains the problem from the other party's side (the client's procurement, the platform's rules). The turn is where the reader's view changes. Limits come before the payoff.
- **Ending**: a judgment one size larger than the opening and more precise, the mechanism more concrete; it answers the opening by returning to the same event or moment; the reader leaves with one judgment and one action only this article could give (a sentence to say, a checklist for the team chat, a first step). If the title promises to name something, the ending names it. Try deleting the last paragraph; if the article gets stronger, delete it. A quotation or a parallel line may close the article when it lands this article's own judgment. Avoid ending on a list of limits, a restated title, an empty slogan, a blessing or a climb to a bigger philosophy.

## The structure editor's questions

Yes, no or "cannot judge", each with one line of evidence quoted from the outline and the opening paragraph.

**The account's good-article standard.** When the configuration names a `good_article_standard`, the organizer pastes its parts on titles, openings and structure (the configuration may name which parts) into prompt 4, and the structure editor answers each of its checks as well, 过、不过 or 判不了 with one quoted line. Checks about how the reader will feel (would I click it, would I forward it) go to the author at stop 2, never to a model.

**First step: the outsider test on the list alone.** Give the structure editor only the title candidates and the subhead list, nothing else. For each line it writes one sentence, "this will teach me ..." (the hard test under "Titles"), and marks every line it cannot put that way and every word an outsider would not understand. Marked lines go back to the writer. After stop 2 the title is the author's choice: if the reader role later reports an unfamiliar word in it, that goes to the author's review sheet, not to the writer (`5-review.md`, Reader).

1. **Article the author chose**: would a reader of the title alone expect this article?
2. **One spine**: can someone who did not see the process say the one sentence this article says? A parallel list with no pull between items fails.
3. **Goes one level down**: is there a moment where the surface answer is overturned?
4. **Scannable**: do the title, subheads and last section alone give the judgment and main moves?
5. **Flow**: does the chain read form one argument; is there a second topic competing with the main one, or a section repeating another?
6. **Arc and ending**: are opening, development, turn and close identifiable; does the ending answer the opening with something usable?
7. **Balance**: sections of similar weight (the audit script reports sizes); none a single thin paragraph, none three times longer than the others.
8. **Evidence budget** as set in `4-writing.md`, including the sentence each outside item proves; "proves nothing" means delete.
9. **Title and subheads**: the hard test, the noun test, the metaphor rule and the wrong-split check pass; the title is answered plainly in the body and the ending; every subhead's promise is delivered by its section.
10. **Opening**: for an opinion piece, does the opening match the judgment (a sharp, unheard one stated head-on; a familiar one held back as a question) and name its subject; a different kind from the account's last few.

A "no" returns to: the outline (questions 2, 5 to 8), the judgment (3), or sentences only (9, 10). Title and subhead points are the author's call: a model's pass is advice, and if the author feels something is off, it does not ship.
