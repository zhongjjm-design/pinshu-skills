# Worked examples

Two demonstration articles from October 2026, from seed to final, including what failed. The article itself is in Chinese and is not bundled; this record keeps the decisions and the fixes.

## Before it: two rejected attempts

- **Attempt one** skipped the judgment and outline steps and went straight from a topic to a draft. The result was hollow: accurate sentences, no point. Lesson: never skip the judgment confirmation.
- **Attempt two** matured a topic about how much companies spend on AI usage. The author rejected the topic itself: whether AI is worth using is no longer a question for these readers. Lesson: "is AI useful" is not a topic.

## Topic

From several cards, the author picked: how a service company (agency, content studio, design shop) keeps the money AI saves it, when clients know it uses AI. The author redirected the angle: stand on the agency's side, and answer how efficiency turns into more income and in what ways. Also: do not mention our own company; write objectively.

## Judgment history

- **Seed**: AI efficiency should become income for agencies, not discounts for clients.
- **Baseline**: models from three vendors gave the standard answers: value-based pricing, raise prices, emphasize creativity. Those are the floor.
- **Widening** found the material that changed the judgment:
  - why clients cut prices: procurement compares bids by headcount times days times day rate, because it is easy to compare and audit; some public-sector contracts even require person-day counts;
  - an agency that advertised "40 percent lower cost" to a pharmaceutical client got the reply "then cut 40 percent";
  - in translation, pricing human post-editing as a separate line turned it into a discount tier;
  - survey data: few agencies had actually cut prices because of AI so far, while many large brands planned to change how they pay.
- **Red team** (another vendor): value-based pricing has retreated among large advertisers; clients can do more in-house; low-price competitors set the floor.
- **Matured judgment**: stop selling speed; split the quote so the client can cut the execution part but not the judgment and quality-control part; price by what is delivered, not by hours; spend the saved time on new income (premium style work, clients who could not afford you before, new products such as training or running AI content systems for clients). State the cases where the price cannot be held.

## Title

Two rounds of eight candidates went through the cold-reader test, each round read by two models from different vendors. The first round's titles were judged vague or low-grade; the second round produced the one the author picked, meaning "Don't let the money AI saves you become your client's discount".

## Draft one: rejected

The author's verdict: stiff from the first line, not plain, the reader gets stuck, the arc is unclear, paragraph lengths uneven, subheads not written for skimmers, the piece feels torn apart. What the audit and review found:

- opening on a news-style fact instead of the reader's own scene;
- sources in parentheses inside almost every paragraph;
- jargon from the research material (person-days, rate cards, pricing anchors, deliverables);
- five parallel "roads" with no pull between them;
- several paragraphs with five or more numbers;
- eight thin sections plus the opening, between about 140 and 410 characters each: too many to carry one argument;
- five single-sentence reversals.

## Rewrite

- Opening: a hypothetical ten-person content studio at contract renewal; the client's marketing director smiles and asks whether the price can come down now that they use AI. The real pharmaceutical case follows immediately as proof.
- Development from the client's side: why procurement thinks in person-days, so the reader understands instead of resenting.
- Sections: why clients cut; split the quote; price by delivery, not by hours; earn new money with the saved time; where the price cannot be held.
- Sources moved to a list after the body.
- Each section about 450 to 700 characters, subheads in plain words.

## Jargon round

The author still found words they did not understand. A layperson scan by a model from another vendor quoted every stuck point; the writer replaced or explained each, and the scan was repeated. After the fixes, the scan's readability score rose to 8 out of 10. The fact check removed approximate figures the writer had invented ("the past two years", "thirty to forty percent") and labeled self-reported company numbers.

## Ending: from weightless to usable

The author passed the draft but asked: the ending stops on "in these situations the price is hard to hold". Is that it? Does the reader get anything from that?

Fix:

1. Move the limits section before the new-income section, so limits come before the payoff.
2. Add a final section, roughly "next time the client asks for a discount, you can say this", returning to the renewal scene with a short script in the owner's own voice: the editing and production part is faster with AI, so that part comes down; topic direction, creative judgment and pre-publication review are still done person by person and we carry the risk, so that part stays; from now on we price per month by pieces delivered and rounds of revision; with the saved time we will add a monthly content review for you.
3. Put the two survey numbers after the script, to say that clients will raise this sooner or later and it is better to sort out the quote first.
4. End on a concrete action: after the renewal talk, send the new quote.

## What this example teaches

- The judgment changed most during widening, when the material came from the other party's side (procurement), not from more supporting cases.
- Readability problems came mainly from material leaking into the text unprocessed: jargon, inline sources and number piles.
- The ending is judged by one question: at the moment in the opening scene, what does the reader now do or say?
- Every number the model reports about its own draft gets checked by the script.

## Second example: judging staff by AI usage

**Topic.** Two rounds of cards were rejected. Round one rearranged recent analysis pieces from leading media; the author said they moved nobody. Round two built cards on the account's own work (our writing tests, our videos, our AI subscriptions); the author called them self-indulgent. The topic that worked came from a plain list of what readers were reading that week (hot lists of leading business and marketing media), from which the author picked "AI keeps getting stronger, so why are companies still not faster?".

**Judgment history.**

- Seed: everyone is faster, so work piles up waiting for a decision, often the boss's. Three models said exactly this unprompted (one even named the theory of constraints). Dropped.
- Second version: judging staff by AI usage produces work that looks finished but is not, and colleagues pay for it. Two red teams broke it: the research shows the problem exists but not that usage targets cause it; the cost (about 3.4 hours a month) is too small to explain a slow company; most small companies never ran usage targets; Amazon and Meta stepping back is a change of phase, not an admission.
- Matured: pushing people to use AI is right at the start; once everyone uses it, keep pushing usage and the company does not get faster. Then judge what each person hands over: can the next person use it directly?

**Evidence used.** Amazon took down its internal AI-usage leaderboard in May 2026 after staff ran pointless tasks to climb it; Meta said in September 2026 that reviews would no longer use AI adoption dashboards or token counts, while 93% of its code changes involved AI; the Stanford and BetterUp "workslop" research (about 40% received such work in a month, about two hours to deal with each); a survey of 5,000 US white-collar workers (92% of executives say AI makes them more productive; 40% of non-managers say it saves no time).

**What the author rejected after the draft passed review.**

- Title "He saved two hours with AI, his colleague spent two more": two model cold readers chose it; the author could not tell who or what. Final: "Stop judging staff by how much AI they use".
- Opening "suppose you are the boss of a company of a few dozen people": the same kind of opening as the previous article. Final: the Amazon leaderboard story.
- Subheads that were half-sentences of different lengths ("colleagues stop daring to use what you hand over"; "do not rush to change these three"). Final: six plain subheads of 9 to 12 characters that tell the argument in order.
- An ending that returned to "the weekly meeting" after the meeting scene had been deleted. Final: "three things the boss can do now", including a three-question checklist to paste into the team chat, closing with the Amazon executive's line turned into "do not make colleagues finish your AI's work".

**What it teaches.** The middle of the article survived every round; all the rework was in the topic and the title points. Spend the effort there, and let the author's eyes decide them.


## Second round: two articles that produced the gates (version 1.1.0)

Two more articles were written on one day with version 1.0.0: one on why the same AI-video prompt produced very different results, one on what remains after a year of chasing AI tools. Almost every rejection happened upstream:

- **Judgment, rejected six rounds running** on one article: old common sense, explained away in a sentence by the reader, no stance, a single small account's three videos taken as the main line. It stood once it punctured the most-repeated claim of the wave ("one prompt makes a finished video") by showing the long specification and production pipeline behind it. The other article found its angle where the mainstream view ("plug the same tools into a stronger model and they get better") disagreed with the evidence (each model generation deleted many of the old rules).
- **Topic cards** sold how many tools had been installed; rejected as boasting. A judgment refined on the spot from the seed proved, after research, to be an angle already written to death.
- **Body**: one outline offered only two concepts and nothing to act on; the other drowned in research figures.
- **Titles and subheads**: a key word read in its other everyday meaning; subheads built on "the first time" and "the first craft" that the body never earned; an abstract title the author called empty.
- **Opening**: an outline opened on "imagine a company" again, after earlier articles had done the same.
- **Ending**: three generic team habits, called soft and unoriginal.
- **Downstream**: one reviewer proposed cutting the author's own experience, another asked to soften the core judgment; neither was followed. The layperson read caught a number that did not add up and a concept never shown, the most useful of three review rounds. When drafts were criticized, offering new subheads or a new outline instead of a revised draft was rejected: the author cannot judge them outside the article.
- **Writer model**: wrote short four times, invented opening scenes, rewrote locked subheads, wrote the author in the third person, and ran out of quota on both articles.

These became the judgment gate, the skeleton gate, the three downstream rules and the writer-habit notes in model routing.
