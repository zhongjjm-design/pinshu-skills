# Topics and judgment

A first idea is a seed, not a conclusion. Whoever has it sees only part of the picture and carries the usual blind spots. Do not treat the seed as a golden sentence and then pile up material to prove it. Research exists to let the judgment grow, not to endorse the first sentence. But growing is not replacing: research sharpens what the author wants to say; it does not swap in a different article.

## Where to start

- Whatever the author brings (a topic and an angle, a situation, a hunch, a long loose message), it is a seed: run seed confirmation (below) and stop at approval 1 before any research. A precise topic only makes the confirmation short; it never skips it.
- The author has no idea yet: show a short plain list of what readers are reading and discussing this week, with the evidence (hot lists of leading business and industry media, most-read and most-saved lists, comment volume), and let the author pick before writing 3 to 5 topic cards. The card the author picks is then confirmed as a seed in the same way, with its own point map.
- The author brings a judgment, an outline or a draft: follow the entry table in SKILL.md ("Three steps to start"); first rebuild the seed and point map from the author's own words and confirm them.
- In testing, the topics that worked came from the author's one-line idea or from such a list; topics assembled from other people's articles, or built on the account's own work, were rejected.

## The seed

### Only the author sets the seed

A seed is what the author said, or something the author explicitly accepted ("yes, write this"). A pattern the agent spots in the material, a card written by another agent or a scheduled task, a news item: these are seed candidates. Show them to the author; they become seeds only when the author accepts them. Never start a test or a rewrite from an agent-written card.

### Seed confirmation

Authors often talk the seed out at length, in no order. Before any research or judgment:

1. **Keep the words.** Store the author's full text verbatim in the article folder. If the author used voice input, list corrected readings of misheard words in a separate table beside it; never edit the original.
2. **Restate in five items**, in the author's own words wherever possible: what the author wants to say; what they want the reader to take away; the experiences, cases and material they mentioned; what they clearly do not want; what is still unclear (at most three questions).
3. **Number the seed points (the point map).** Every distinct point the author made gets a number: S1, S2, S3 ... (for example "effort used to be visible as hours; now everyone's output looks the same", "the cost of doing went down but money did not get easier to earn", "what separates people now is their ceiling"). The point map follows the article to the end: the judgment card says what happened to each point, and the final review checks the finished article against it. A long seed with eight points has eight numbers; do not merge them to make the list look tidy. Anything the author wrote as a list (a run of questions, numbered items, a series of checks) gets one number per item unless the author allows merging; write the count check under the map: "the author listed N items here; the map has N numbers", and name anything unmatched. In testing, a model turned the author's ten questions into "eight dimensions", and another folded four checks into one number.
4. **List the author's own material separately.** Two kinds: passages that read like lived experience or carry a picture (a scene, a person, a moment), and insights only this author could have (from their business, clients, years in the trade). List each with the author's words and ask: can this be used in the article, and is there a real story behind it?
5. **Opinion pieces need the author's own material.** If the seed is for an opinion piece and the author has given no lived experience or first-hand material, stop and ask for one real story before drafting. If the author says "there is none, write it anyway", write it, and put this line on the review sheet: "fails good-article question 5 (a part only this author could write)". In testing, a piece written with no author experience at all read as a pile of research.
6. **Decide the article type with the author here**: practical how-to, opinion, method, news analysis, integration piece, or another type (SKILL.md's workflow table names the file with the full list). The type decides how the judgment, the evidence and the subheads work later. If the author said "not big ideas, real usable steps", the type is practical how-to and stays so.
7. **Who benefits first.** Ask, or note from the seed, who the article's first beneficiary is. For a practical piece built from other people's successful methods, the first beneficiary is often the author's own team: then also produce a separate internal improvement list (what we do, where we fall short, what to try), which never goes into the article.
8. **Ask "is this what you mean?"** This is condensing, not refining (the approval 1 message opens by restating the seed in the author's words, never with a refined judgment or a "from A to B" framing): do not pick one line as the main point, add a judgment, or drop anything the author said; anything you leave out of the restatement, list as "not included, tell me if it matters". Run the same `--card --seed` check on the restatement before it is shown; a flag means the restatement changed the author's scope, so fix it to the author's words. Revise until the author confirms. The confirmed restatement and point map, not your reading of the long text, are the seed for everything that follows.

Until the research pack and the collision scan are done, show the author only "the seed, plus the questions we will check"; never a refined judgment. In testing, a judgment refined on the spot from the seed turned out, after research, to be an angle already written to death.

### The direction is the anchor

A judgment has two layers, and research may touch only one of them.

- **The direction** (the anchor): what the author wants to do with this article, who it is for, and what kind of article it is. This comes from the confirmed seed. Research may not change it on its own: when the evidence overturns the core (below), the agent proposes one new direction and the author decides.
- **The wording and the claims inside that direction**: how exactly the point is said, which cases carry it, where it stops being true. Research, the common-sense floor and the red team exist to sharpen this layer: make the claim more precise, mark its limits, find a better angle and harder evidence within the author's direction.

In testing, an author asked for a practical how-to piece ("real, usable steps, not big ideas"). After searching, the agent decided the original idea was too common and turned it into an opinion piece; a red-team objection then reshaped it again. It took three rounds to get back to what the author wanted. Later, four sessions writing the same seed with four different models produced four articles; three had quietly narrowed or replaced the seed (one saw that a business magazine had covered a related topic and narrowed the subject; another found a point "already common" and squeezed the author's ten-item observation list into one line). The research was right about the facts and wrong to steer.

### What research can do to a seed: three outcomes

When research is done, the seed ends in exactly one of three states. Name the state on the judgment card.

| Outcome | How you know | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Stands** (the most common) | The material supports it, or others have written about it, or some people disagree | Keep the direction. Use the material to make the claim precise, add evidence, and write the limits. |
| **Partly wrong** | Reliable evidence shows one part does not hold | Keep the direction. Fix the part that is wrong and say in the article under what conditions it does not hold. |
| **Core overturned** | Several independent, recent, first-hand sources directly contradict the seed's central claim | Stop. Show the author the evidence and one proposed new direction. The author decides. |

**These do not count as overturning:** someone else has written on the topic (a collision); the mainstream view is different; the red team argued forcefully but brought no evidence; a model's common-sense answer. The red team's job is to find holes and limits, not to set the direction.

### Two tests whenever the direction or the core claim would change

Whether the change comes from the agent, the red team or the evidence, run both tests and show the result to the author with the proposal.

1. **Against simply obeying.** Written exactly as the seed says, what new thing does the reader get? If the seed says something everyone already knows, the value must come from one of three places: the author's own experience, an integration of the best that others have done, or a method the reader can follow. If none of the three is available, say plainly "written as it is, this will be flat" and ask the author what to do; do not force out an empty draft.
2. **Against contradicting for its own sake.** Put the new direction beside the original and compare four things: does the reader care, what can the reader do after reading, would the author sign it, is there material only this author has. If the new direction is only more novel and loses on these four, keep the original.

A draft that flatters the seed into a worthless article fails; so does a draft that refutes the seed into something correct and dull.

### Collisions: four layers, three paths

Many articles on the same subject mean the topic is hot and readers want it. That is not a reason to drop or narrow the seed. Sort every collision into one of four layers:

1. same subject;
2. same question;
3. same judgment;
4. same structure, evidence and conclusion (one layer: all three must match together; the same structure or evidence with a different conclusion is not layer 4).

Layers 1 and 2 are normal; keep going. Only layers 3 and 4 raise the question of a different angle, and then choose one of three paths:

1. **We have our own angle or the author's experience**: write our own piece.
2. **We have neither**: write an integration piece: take the best of the most-read, best-performing articles on the subject, credit each one, and add our own judgment and choices (article type 10).
3. **Neither of those works either**: only then consider dropping or replacing the topic. The author decides.

Report the result as a count for every layer, zeros included: layer 1: N, layer 2: N, layer 3: N, layer 4: N, could not be placed: N. In testing, a model reported layers 1, 2 and 4 and said nothing about layer 3, the one that decides whether a new angle is needed.

If the collision scan finds the account's own earlier piece on the same subject, first ask whether this is the next piece in a series.

### How this standard grows

This is the first version of the seed standard. Every article's feedback candidates record one line: did the seed stand, change in part, or get overturned, and did the author agree with how it was handled. After several articles, revisit the table above.

## What makes a topic worth writing

The reader is already asking about it or cares about it, and the article gives at least one of these (more is better):

| What the reader feels | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "It said what I felt but could not put into words" | Resonance. People share an article because it speaks for them. |
| "It showed me what I had not seen" | The reader believed A; after reading, sees B. |
| "I can use this" | It changes a decision, or gives a step the reader can take tomorrow. |
| "Worth forwarding" | Sharing it makes the sender look informed, or helps a colleague or client. |

The judgment does not have to be one nobody else could reach. It has to be valuable to the reader and genuinely the author's own. Topics can collide with other writers; angles cannot.

Do not use topics that pull readers with greed or fear. They damage the tone that sponsors and serious readers value.

"Is AI useful?" is not a topic. The reader has moved past it; the question is what to do now.

Two failure patterns from testing:

- **Rearranged articles.** Cards assembled from other writers' recent analysis pieces read as competent and moved nobody, including the author. Other people's articles are a trigger, not a source. (An integration piece is different: it is chosen openly, credits every source and adds our own judgment.)
- **Our own work as the subject.** "What we built", "what we tested", "what our videos taught us" fails the subject check: the reader asks why they should care. Use the account's own work only as evidence inside a reader's problem, or when the reader would pay to see that process.

## Topic card

Give the author 3 to 5 cards. Each card has:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| One-line topic | The question the reader is asking, in the reader's words |
| Source | Which of the account's sources it came from: the author's idea, the author's business, the author's own work, library material, what readers are reading now, reader questions |
| Reader-interest evidence | Why we believe readers care this week: a hot-list position, read or save counts, comment volume, or the author's own observation |
| Value | Which of the four reader feelings it delivers |
| Belief shift | "The reader thinks ___; after reading, finds ___" (the first half must be something real readers believe) |
| Gap | What existing coverage misses; our provisional view; where informed people disagree |
| Collision list | The main argument of the top 5 to 10 existing articles on this topic, the collision layer of each (1 to 4), and what we add. Include the account's own work: published articles are read in full; its drafts and ready-to-publish pieces are checked by title only. A quick scan at card stage; without web access, write "not scanned" and do the scan in the research pack. Articles recalled from model memory go under "leads to check", never into this list or any layer |
| Who will care | Why like-minded readers would forward it; which budget holders (brands, PR and media agencies) would notice |
| Middle layer | For business and brand topics: the business logic between the product and the financials |
| Biggest risk | The likeliest way this topic turns out flat or wrong |

Before showing any card, run the common-sense floor on its "after reading" line (two cheap models from different vendors are enough) and drop cards whose line the models already say unprompted. Read the full text of the closest existing article; a title scan misses angles already covered. Then check each remaining card against the account profile's topic gate. A generic floor if the profile has none: original viewpoint, professional analysis, new information. Put failing cards last with a one-line reason; the author may rescue one. If nothing passes, say so. Better no topic than a mediocre one. You may mark one card as your recommendation with a one-line reason.

## Research pack

- Start from the configured source list (leading business, technology and brand media; the author's own notes), then search the last three months. If the agent cannot browse, write "no web access" plainly.
- **The author's own material comes first.** If the author mentions conversations, notes or posts they collected earlier ("we have a conversation specifically about this"), locate those first and extract what the author shared there, back to the original posts, before any keyword search. Folder settings in the configuration do not cover material left in other conversations; ask where it is if you cannot find it.
- Search in three lanes: collision (who has written this angle, with the collision layer of each; the account's own published articles are read in full, its drafts and ready-to-publish pieces by title only), cases for and against, and opposing evidence. If your tool can dispatch helpers, run the three in parallel; otherwise run them one after another yourself. Each lane states whether it read the full source or only a fragment, and writes "not found" when it finds nothing.
- Anything a helper, another conversation or another AI passes on (and any new material we write into revision notes ourselves) goes back to its original source before use; for papers, check the latest version.
- Each item: date, source, **its link and the exact sentence it is quoted for** (so a screenshot can later be taken straight from the list, without searching again), what it supports, and its status: independent report, company self-report, vendor press release, speaker's own account, or repost of an original not yet opened. Also state how it was read: **original read in full** (only when the page text itself was fetched), **fragment only** (search-tool results count here, however long), or **paraphrased by a tool** (many fetch tools return a model's summary of the page, so quotes are not verbatim; never quote from a paraphrase without checking the original).
- Sort newest first. Factual evidence older than six months is either replaced or its year goes into the text. Classic principles are exempt. Claims about AI tools must be current.
- When the author names a source, find the original and read the fullest version. Record which parts were used and which were not; "used" does not mean "used fully".
- If something cannot be found, write that it cannot be found. Never fill a date or number from memory.
- If only headlines could be reached (for example articles whose text could not be fetched), say so; the collision scan then counts as incomplete.
- When the author asks for a blind test ("do not read earlier drafts"), the collision scan and the opening comparison read titles and first paragraphs only, and say so.
- After the lanes return, merge them into one short research summary (the strongest items with status) on top of the lane files, so the author and the writer read one page.
- Without web access, items recalled from model memory may be listed under "leads to check", labeled "from model memory, not verified". They guide the thinking but do not enter the article as facts until verified against a source, and they never enter the collision scan: no layer, no part in question 6.

### Fact table

By approval 3 (before drafting), lock every name, number, quote (with its Chinese wording), date and author experience the article may use into one fact table. The writer may use only the table. Two kinds of entry are copied as whole sentences, never paraphrased:

- the author's own experiences and statements, taken word for word from the author's messages and the decision log (anything missing is marked "[to confirm]" and asked);
- key statements quoted from the account's own earlier articles: the whole sentence, with the article title and date.

Each external entry keeps its link and quoted sentence from the research pack. When the table is locked at approval 3, mark the entries that need an evidence screenshot; the screenshot job captures from that column (delivery reference, "Evidence screenshots").

**The table stays complete.** Whenever a fact is checked or re-measured later (in review, in a revision, on publishing day), write it into the table at once with its source and date. Before the editor-in-chief review, check that every fact in the draft is in the table. In testing, a final review reported 15 "facts not in the material", nearly all of them checked earlier in the conversation but never written back.

## Judgment maturation: five steps

1. **Seed.** Run seed confirmation (above). The output is the confirmed restatement, the point map and the author's own material list.
2. **Baseline: what everyone already knows.**
   - Collision list from leading media and peers, each sorted into its layer.
   - Common-sense floor: ask two or three models from different vendors "what is the mainstream view" (prompt in the prompt kit). What they answer by default is common sense; the article must go beyond it.
   - Reader voices: comment sections, Q&A sites, questions the author hears from owners and marketers.
3. **Widen: look for material that could sharpen or limit the judgment**, not only material that supports it. Material that could change the direction is handled by the three outcomes above; it never changes the direction by itself.
   - First-hand facts and data.
   - Different positions: supporters, opponents, bystanders, and the people who pay (for a service business, the client's procurement team).
   - Cut from different directions: comparison, distinction, incentives, anomalies, second-order effects. Each cut may yield a different candidate wording.
   - Argue the other side: state the strongest opposing case so well that its holder would accept it. Then ask a model from another vendor to assume the article has already failed and list where it is most likely to be proven wrong (prompt in the prompt kit). The red team finds holes and limits; it does not set the direction.
   - Four ways to find the angle, all tried in testing or taken from proven method notes:
     - **Where the mainstream view and the evidence disagree.** Take the common-sense floor answers and check them one by one against the material; look for "everyone says this, the evidence points the other way".
     - **Puncture the most-repeated line of a hype wave.** Find the sentence everyone forwards and check what actually stands behind it. In testing, a viral "one sentence produces the finished work" claim turned out to rest on a hidden specification of thousands of words and a whole production pipeline; that gap became the article.
     - **Word archaeology.** Take a word readers use every day; trace which conditions made it true when it appeared, which condition has since been lost, and what wrong decision someone made because of that loss.
     - **Two tests for going against the consensus.** The line you argue against must be one real readers believe, and enough of them; after knocking it down, solve the problem again so the reader knows what to do instead.
   - When the author's seed is a hype wave, the main line is the wave: what it brings and what it means for the reader. A single case (one account, one product, three videos) is evidence inside it, never the main line or the title.
4. **Discuss with the author, one or two rounds.** This happens at approval 2: the judgment card opens the discussion, the author responds, the card is revised. It adds no extra stop.
   - Present "you originally thought X; the material shows Y and Z; the other side says W; this is what I recommend", not a finished conclusion.
   - The author talks; the agent condenses. The agent may also put forward its own view to test against.
   - After each round, log one line: how the judgment changed. This log is the article's judgment history.
5. **Mature, then outline.** A judgment is mature enough when:
   - it fits in one sentence (one main line; it may have several ranked dimensions that answer the same question);
   - you can say when it would not hold;
   - it answers the strongest objection;
   - it delivers at least one of the four reader feelings;
   - the author believes it and will sign it.

   If it is not there yet, keep growing it or return the topic to the pool.

## Judgment gate (before the judgment card goes to the author)

The five maturity tests above were the first standard. In later testing, six successive judgments passed all of them and were still rejected by the author as empty. So the judgment must also pass this gate before it is shown. No outline is written until it passes.

**The relationship sentence.** Write the judgment as one relationship: "under condition C, A changes B through mechanism M, so result R follows". If you cannot write it, do not lay out a body. Then try the reader's laziest reading of it on the smallest possible example and see where it breaks; repair one concept at a time.

**Seven questions.** A failing answer on any one sends the judgment back, through the table that follows:

1. **Is it only old common sense with nothing new attached?** A classic principle may be the subject; classic judgments are not judged by age. The question is what the new situation (AI, the triggering event) changed about it: a condition it depends on, a new consequence, new evidence or a new way of acting. If none of these is in the judgment, it fails. (Rejected example: a claim about craft quality stated exactly as it would have been before the new tool existed, with nothing about what the tool changed.)
2. **Can the reader dismiss it in one sentence?** Being summarizable in a sentence is not a fault; good judgments often are. It fails only if the reader's one-sentence reply ("different tools give different results", "obviously") leaves nothing for the article to do. (Rejected example: a puzzle the reader answers at once with "different tools give different results".)
3. Does it only describe, with no stance? Does it make the task sound harder and scare the reader off? (Rejected example: "doing this job has now become programming": no stance, and it sounds harder.)
4. Is its selling point "how much we installed or built"? (Rejected example: three topic cards selling the number of tools the author had installed.)
5. (Opinion, method and news pieces only; skip for practical how-to and integration pieces.) Is it only a method or a checklist, with no "I thought A; it is actually B" for the reader? (Rejected example: "why these tools matter and which tasks suit them"; readers already know.)
6. How many articles took this same angle in the last six months, counting the account's own published pieces (full text) and its drafts and ready-to-publish pieces (titles), and at which collision layer? Answer from the collision scan in the research pack, and paste that scan into prompt kit 10; a model's memory is not a scan. Only layers 3 and 4 count against the judgment. Every answer states how complete the scan was: how many read in full, fragment only, title only, and whether the account's own published pieces were scanned (drafts and ready pieces read by title only is the rule, not a gap). Without a scan, or with an incomplete one, the answer is "unverified", never "none" or "passed"; the gate stays open on this question, and the approval 2 message says "collisions unverified" so the author decides whether to scan more or go on with the gap. An unverified answer is still no reason to narrow the seed or change the topic.
7. Does it lack any of four things: important, new, true, said with enough force? "Enough force" means the sentence still stands after removing every "maybe" and "to some extent"; keep only as many qualifiers as your certainty requires. (Paul Graham, "How to Write Usefully", read in full.)

**What to do after a failure.** The gate gives a next step, not only a verdict.

| Failed | Next step |
|---|---|
| 1 Nothing new attached | Find what AI or the event changed: a condition, a consequence, evidence, a way of acting. If nothing changed, it may still work as a classic reread (type 7) or an integration piece; ask the author. |
| 2 Dismissed in one sentence | Go one level below the reader's reply: what does that reply miss, and what decision goes wrong because of it? |
| 3 No stance, or scares the reader | State what the reader should do or stop doing; turn "harder" into "where to start". |
| 4 Sells our effort | Recast around the reader's problem; our work becomes evidence inside it. |
| 5 Only a method | Name what the reader believed before and what is true instead; or, if the author wants a method piece, confirm the type. |
| 6 Collision at layer 3 or 4 | Use the three paths under "Collisions": our own angle or experience, an integration piece, or (author's call) a different topic. |
| 7 Missing importance, novelty, truth or force | Name which one; for truth, more evidence; for force, cut qualifiers; for importance or novelty, back to step 3 or to the author. |
| Breaks down (only destroys, builds nothing) | Add the new standard or the new way of doing it; the reader must leave knowing what to do instead. |

If any question fails, stop and rethink with the author; do not force a draft. If the judgment changes after the red team or the gate, run the gate (and the red team, if the change is substantial) again on the new version. Record each answer with its evidence; an all-pass record with no evidence is not a pass.

**Who answers the seven questions.** Where possible, have a model from another vendor answer them too (prompt kit 10) and show both. When the writer and the organizer are the same model (for example, the author named the main conversation as writer), the seven questions must be answered by a model from another vendor; the organizer may not answer its own questions alone. If no other vendor can be called, answer them in a fresh session, record "same-vendor review", and say in the approval 2 message that the same model wrote and checked the judgment; the author decides whether to go on (see the one-model fallback in the model routing guide).

**After two rejections in a row**, stop guessing new wordings. Ask the author for the one sentence they want readers to remember, and build from it. If the author explicitly says "think again" or "rewrite", do that, and ask for the sentence in the same reply. In testing, a judgment rejected six rounds running drifted further from the author's intent with every rewording.

## Judgment card (what the author approves at approval 2)

The card is kept in the article folder; the chat message follows the approval-message shape in the approvals reference.

- The matured judgment in one sentence, in plain words, plus its relationship sentence.
- **The seed's outcome**: stands, partly wrong, or core overturned (with the evidence if overturned).
- **Seed against judgment**: a table with the author's own sentences on the left and the judgment's on the right; columns: kept, added, removed or changed, needs the author's approval. List every scope or relationship word that changed (still, also, only, all, no longer, replaces, the one, has failed) on its own line. Then run the audit script on the card with the author's verbatim seed (`--card --seed <seed file>`): it flags scope wording the seed does not use (no longer, turn to, replace, the real thing is, the only, has failed, worthless, go back to, do not look at). Every flag goes to the author at approval 2; the model's own table never overrides the script. The outcome may not be written as "stands", and the card may not go to approval 2, until this table and that check are done. In testing, two models turned the author's "these still count, and we also look at" into "these no longer count" and "there is only one new measure", and one then wrote that the direction had not changed.
- **The point map, point by point**: one line for each seed point S1, S2 ...: kept, deepened, split into another article, or dropped, with one line why. Never sum up ("S1 to S28 all kept", "the rest unchanged"), even when everything is kept. Dropping a point needs the author's explicit approval. Any change of direction, article type or reader is marked for the author to decide.
- **The author's own material**: which items the article will use and where.
- The judgment gate record: each of the seven questions with a one-line answer and its evidence.
- Judgment history: seed, each change, and why.
- Strongest objection and the answer; the conditions where the judgment fails.
- Evidence list: the three to five strongest items with dates and status.
- Reader: who exactly, where they are stuck, what they will do differently.
- Proposed article type and the opening scene.
- Gaps: missing facts or author experience (ask at most three questions).

## Practical pieces

A practical how-to piece ("how to do X, step by step") works differently from an opinion piece, and in testing every opinion-oriented check pushed a practical seed toward an opinion article.

- **The judgment is a thread, not a thesis.** It is one sentence that ties the steps together (for example, "the gap is in the preparation, not the model"), not a claim to defend. The judgment gate's question 5 does not apply, and questions 1 and 2 test whether the steps are new to the reader, not whether the thread is.
- **The judgment card becomes a route table.** Rows are the steps; columns: what the step is, who has actually done it (named practitioners who showed their work), source, whether we have done it ourselves, and where we fall short. Steps included in the article must have been done by someone and be sourced; single-source steps are marked optional. The point map still applies.
- **Every step of a framework the agent assembled is labeled.** If the agent merges several people's steps into one route, each step shows which sources mention it, who showed a finished result, and whether we have tried it. Otherwise neither the author nor the reader can tell which steps were invented by the merge.
- **Evidence standard**: "someone has walked this route and it is sourced", not "proven effective". Do not pad the card with statements of evidence strength that the author does not need.
- **Subheads**: the action plus why it matters, as described in the plain-writing reference.
- **The author's own rejected drafts and comments** are internal material; they never appear in the article as evidence.
